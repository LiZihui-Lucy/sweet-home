My first encounter with programming did not happen in a computer science classroom. I studied architectural design as an undergraduate, and I first encountered programming through parametric design. Using Grasshopper, a visual programming environment widely used in architectural design, I began to understand that a design did not always have to start with a fixed final form. Instead of directly modelling an object that I had already imagined, I could define parameters, relationships, and rules, then allow these elements to generate different possible outcomes.

This experience changed my understanding of what design could be. Before learning about parametric design, I usually approached a project by imagining a form and gradually developing it into a final outcome. Although the design might change during the process, I was still generally moving towards a specific result. Computational approaches to design, however, introduced a different logic. Parametric and generative design focus not only on producing a form, but also on establishing the relationships and rules through which forms can be generated (Caetano et al., 2020). I began to see that I did not always need to design the final outcome directly. I could design the system that produced it.

I experienced this most clearly in one of my installation projects. The project was composed of several modular components that could be combined in different ways. Rather than determining one final arrangement at the beginning, I used programming to establish relationships and rules for how the modules could be organised. Parameters and random combinations allowed the system to produce multiple possible arrangements.

The final installation was therefore not entirely based on a form that I had imagined in advance. Instead, it emerged from the interaction between relatively simple components, parameters, and rules. This was the first time I clearly understood the creative potential of programming. A small number of elements and relatively simple rules could produce results that were much more complex and unpredictable than the original system appeared to suggest.

This idea strongly connects with Dylan Beattie's The Art of Code. Beattie presents code not only as a technical method for solving practical problems, but also as a medium for experimentation, play, and creative expression. His talk demonstrates how computational systems can create surprising complexity and unexpected results from relatively simple structures. My installation project helped me understand this idea through practice. The interesting part of the project was not simply creating one successful final form. It was discovering what could emerge from a system when its rules and parameters began to interact.

At first, allowing randomness into my design process felt like giving up control. As a designer, I was used to thinking that I should be responsible for every decision in the final work. However, programming made me reconsider what authorship meant. I did not decide every final arrangement, but I still designed the modular elements, determined the parameters, and established the rules that defined how the system could operate. Giving up complete control over the final outcome did not mean giving up authorship. Instead, my role shifted from directly producing every result to defining the conditions under which results could emerge.

This relationship between humans and computational systems can also be connected to Ada Lovelace's famous discussion of the Analytical Engine. Lovelace wrote that the Analytical Engine had “no pretensions whatever to originate anything” and could perform what humans knew how to order it to perform (Lovelace, 1843). Although my installation project involved randomness and produced outcomes that I could not completely predict, the system itself did not exist independently from my decisions. Its possibilities were shaped by the rules, components, and relationships that I had established. For me, this suggests that programming does not necessarily remove the designer from the creative process. Instead, it can relocate creative decisions to another level: the design of systems.

This way of thinking also changed how I understand design more broadly. Before encountering programming, one of my main questions was often: What should the final form look like? After working with parametric design, I began asking different questions: What elements should exist in this system? How should they relate to one another? Which variables should remain fixed, and which should be allowed to change? What kinds of outcomes might emerge from their interaction?

In other words, programming shifted my attention from designing objects to designing relationships. I was no longer only designing a result; I was designing the conditions from which results could emerge.

This way of thinking has also influenced how I understand game design. A game can contain many different elements, including narrative, visual design, sound, characters, and world-building. Any one of these elements may make a game memorable, but they do not necessarily make it enjoyable to play. Gameplay depends on the interaction between rules, players, actions, and consequences.

This is particularly clear in many board games. Their basic rules may be relatively simple, but when these rules interact with different players and different decisions, they can create unexpected situations, strategies, and experiences. A game designer cannot determine every moment that a player will experience. Instead, they create a system in which interesting moments can emerge.

I began to recognise a connection between this logic and my experience with parametric design. In my installation project, modular components and rules produced multiple possible forms. In a game, rules and player interactions can produce multiple possible experiences. In both cases, the designer is not responsible for directly creating every possible outcome. Instead, the designer creates a structure that makes those outcomes possible.

This is why I think learning programming is meaningful for me as a designer. I am not here simply because programming is a useful technical skill, or because designers may need to understand technology in the future. For me, programming has introduced a different way of thinking about design.

It has taught me to move from designing fixed outcomes towards designing systems; from focusing only on forms towards understanding relationships; and from controlling every result towards creating the conditions in which new results can emerge. As my interests continue to move beyond architecture towards interaction and game design, I believe this way of thinking will become increasingly important.

Perhaps programming will continue to change, and many of the specific tools or languages taught today may eventually become obsolete. However, the way programming has changed my understanding of design is less likely to disappear. I am learning not only how to make something, but how to construct the rules, relationships, and systems from which something unexpected might emerge.

That is why I am here.
