My first encounter with programming did not happen in a computer science classroom. I studied architectural design as an undergraduate, and I first encountered programming through parametric design. Using Grasshopper, a visual programming environment widely used in architectural design, I began to understand that a design did not always have to start with a fixed final form. Instead of directly modelling an object that I had already imagined, I could define parameters, relationships, and rules, then allow these elements to generate different possible outcomes.

This changed how I understood design. Previously, I would imagine a form and gradually develop it towards a specific result. Computational design introduced a different logic: parametric and generative design focus not only on producing forms, but also on establishing the relationships and rules through which forms can be generated (Caetano et al., 2020). I began to see that I could design the system that produced an outcome rather than designing the outcome directly.

I experienced this most clearly in an installation project made from modular components. Instead of deciding on one arrangement in advance, I used programming to define how the modules could be combined. Parameters and random combinations generated multiple possible arrangements. The final installation therefore emerged from the interaction between simple components, rules, and variables. This showed me that a small system could produce results more complex and unpredictable than it initially appeared.

This experience connects strongly with Dylan Beattie's The Art of Code. Beattie presents code not only as a technical tool, but also as a medium for experimentation, play, and creative expression. His examples show how simple computational structures can create surprising complexity. Similarly, the most interesting part of my installation was not producing one predetermined form, but discovering what could emerge when the system's rules interacted.

This relationship between humans and computational systems can also be connected to Ada Lovelace's famous discussion of the Analytical Engine. Lovelace wrote that the Analytical Engine had “no pretensions whatever to originate anything” and could perform what humans knew how to order it to perform (Lovelace, 1843). Although my installation project involved randomness and produced outcomes that I could not completely predict, the system itself did not exist independently from my decisions. Its possibilities were shaped by the rules, components, and relationships that I had established. For me, this suggests that programming does not necessarily remove the designer from the creative process. Instead, it can relocate creative decisions to another level: the design of systems.

At first, introducing randomness felt like giving up control. I was used to being responsible for every decision in a final design. Programming made me reconsider authorship. Although I did not choose every arrangement, I designed the components, selected the parameters, and established the rules. My role shifted from directly producing every result to defining the conditions under which results could emerge. Losing complete control did not mean losing authorship; it moved authorship to the level of system design.

This way of thinking has influenced my understanding of game design. A game may include narrative, visuals, sound, characters, and world-building, but these elements alone do not determine whether it is enjoyable to play. Gameplay emerges from the interaction between rules, player actions, and consequences. Board games make this especially clear: simple rules can produce unexpected strategies and situations when different players make different choices. A game designer cannot determine every moment a player will experience; they create a system in which interesting moments can emerge.

I see a direct connection between this process and my installation. Modular components and rules produced multiple possible forms, while game rules and player interactions produce multiple possible experiences. In both cases, the designer does not create every outcome directly. Instead, the designer creates a structure that makes those outcomes possible.

For this reason, learning programming is meaningful to me. It has taught me to move from designing fixed outcomes towards designing systems, from focusing only on forms towards understanding relationships, and from controlling every result towards creating conditions for new results to emerge. As my interests develop from architecture towards interaction and game design, this way of thinking will become increasingly important. The tools and languages I learn may change, but the underlying lesson will remain: I am learning not only how to make something, but how to construct the rules and systems from which something unexpected can emerge.

That is why I am here.
